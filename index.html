Abstract

Key Information

Models: Random Forest, XGBoost, Gradient Boosting

Methodology: 100 iterations, 50/50 random balancing

Bankruptcy Prediction Machine Learning Random Forest European Markets Sovereign Debt Financial Distress

This paper replicates and adapts the bankruptcy prediction methodology proposed by Lombardo et al. (2022) to the European listed equity market, evaluating whether their machine learning framework maintains its predictive capacity in a substantially different institutional and macroeconomic context.

An original dataset has been constructed comprising 4,408 listed companies across eight European countries with 91,774 firm-year observations for the period 2004–2024. A total of 76 bankruptcy events were manually identfied through cross-referencing national insolvency registers. The model incorporates the 18 financial variables from Lombardo et al. (2022) augmented with four European-specific variables: interest expense, ESG score, GDP growth and sovereign debt-to-GDP ratio.

The best-performing model in default prediction is Random Forest with WL=3, achieving an AUC of 0.801 on the test set and correctly detecting 9 out of 11 real bankruptcies. In survival probability prediction, Gradient Boosting with LAW=3 achieves an AUC of 0.811, demonstrating that bankruptcies can be anticipated three years in advance. Macroeconomic variables, particularly sovereign debt-to-GDP and GDP growth, are consistently among the most predictive features in the best-performing models, confirming that European-specific contextual variables add genuine predictive signal beyond the original financial ratios.

Results

Models were trained over 100 iterations with random 50/50 balancing. The best model per configuration was selected on the validation set and evaluated on the test set. Logistic Regression was trained but excluded from the main analysis due to convergence failures caused by the extreme class imbalance (76 bankruptcies out of 91,774 observations). AUC by Window Length — Task T1

Mean AUC on validation set across 100 training iterations.

What is AUC?

AUC measures the model's ability to distinguish bankrupt from healthy firms. A value of 0.5 equals random guessing; 1.0 is perfect. An AUC of 0.757 means the model correctly ranks a bankrupt firm above a healthy firm in 75.7% of random pairwise comparisons.

Lombardo benchmark

The dashed line shows AUC=0.748, reported by Lombardo et al. (2022) for Random Forest WL=1 in the US market. It serves as an external reference point. Our model surpasses this benchmark from WL=4 onwards with Random Forest.

Interpretation

All three models improve as Window Length increases, confirming that incorporating more years of historical data helps capture gradual financial deterioration. Random Forest peaks at WL=4 (0.757), while Gradient Boosting continues improving through WL=5 (0.756).

AUC by Look-Ahead Window — Task T2

Mean AUC on validation set. LAW=5 excluded from test analysis.

What is AUC?

AUC measures the model's ability to distinguish bankrupt from healthy firms across all possible decision thresholds. Values above 0.7 are generally considered good for bankruptcy prediction with heavily imbalanced datasets.

What is Look-Ahead Window?

LAW controls how many years in advance the model predicts bankruptcy. LAW=3 means the model uses data from year t to predict whether the firm will go bankrupt in year t+3, giving investors and creditors actionable lead time.

Interpretation

LAW=3 emerges as the optimal horizon across all models. XGBoost peaks sharply at LAW=3 then drops. Random Forest improves steadily. This suggests European bankruptcy signals are detectable and actionable 3 years in advance — consistent with Lombardo et al. (2022) for the US market.

What is variable importance?

Measures how much each variable contributes to the model's predictions on average, calculated as the mean reduction in Gini impurity across all trees (Random Forest and Gradient Boosting) or mean gain (XGBoost).

Red vs blue

Red bars are the four European-specific variables added in this study: sovereign debt-to-GDP, GDP growth, interest expense and ESG score. Blue bars are the original 18 variables from Lombardo et al. (2022). The colour is consistent across all three model panels.

Interpretation

In Random Forest and Gradient Boosting, the two best-performing models, European macroeconomic variables consistently rank among the top predictors. Sovereign debt-to-GDP leads in Gradient Boosting (24.7%) and GDP growth ranks second in Random Forest (12.4%). XGBoost shows a different pattern where accounting variables dominate, with ESG score as the only European variable in the top three.

ROC Curve — Random Forest WL=3

Test set (2020–2024). AUC = 0.801.

What is the ROC curve?

The ROC curve shows the trade-off between detecting real bankruptcies (True Positive Rate / Recall) and generating false alarms (False Positive Rate) across all possible decision thresholds. Each point on the curve represents a different threshold setting.

The diagonal

The dashed diagonal represents a random classifier with AUC=0.500: equivalent to flipping a coin. The further the curve bows towards the upper-left corner, the better the model's discriminatory power.

Interpretation

Random Forest WL=3 achieves AUC=0.801, correctly detecting 9 out of 11 real bankruptcies in the 2020–2024 test period (Recall=81.8%, Type II error=18.2%). The step shape reflects the small number of positive cases in the test set (11 bankruptcies), which is characteristic of highly imbalanced real-world datasets.

Bankruptcy Events by Year (2004–2024)

Distribution of the 76 manually identified bankruptcy events.

What does this show?

The number of bankruptcy events identified per year across the 8 European markets studied (UK, Sweden, France, Germany, Italy, Spain, Switzerland and the Netherlands), covering the period 2004–2024.

Two peaks

2012 (9 events) corresponds to the peak of the European sovereign debt crisis, when countries like Spain, Italy and Portugal faced severe fiscal constraints and banking sector stress. 2018 (8 events) reflects the end of the post-crisis expansion cycle, when several overleveraged firms reached their limits.

Interpretation

The temporal clustering of bankruptcies around macroeconomic stress periods provides empirical justification for incorporating sovereign debt-to-GDP and GDP growth as predictive variables. A purely accounting-based model would miss this country-level systemic dimension of bankruptcy risk.
